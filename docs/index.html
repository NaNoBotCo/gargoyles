Gargoyle or grotesque?

A gargoyle is a drain: water goes in at the roof and out of its mouth. A carved creature that carries no water is a grotesque, or on Notre-Dame, a chimera. Look, guess, then see.

Gargoyle

A spout carved as a creature. A groove or pipe runs through it and the water leaves by the mouth.

Grotesque

Any odd carved figure, human or beast, that carries no water. The word comes from the Italian grotta.

Chimera

The name for the grotesques on Notre-Dame's upper gallery, after the many-part monster of Greek myth.

Cousins

Guardians at doors and on ridges elsewhere: Thai yaksha and singha, Chinese ridge beasts, Japanese ogre tiles, Kala faces.

Throw it clear

Water that runs down a wall washes out the mortar between the stones. A gargoyle sticks out so the water falls free. Make the spout longer or shorter, make it rain harder, and watch where the water lands.

water inQ = rain × 40 m²

The roof gathers the rain falling on 40 square metres and sends it down one spout.

at the lipv = √(g·d), d = (q²/g)^⅓

Water leaving the end of an open channel flows at its critical depth d. Here q is the flow per metre of a 12 cm groove.

fallingx = v × √(2h/g)

From 10 m up, the water falls for about 1.4 seconds. In that time it drifts out x metres more.

Even in a downpour the water leaves the lip slowly, well under a metre a second, and falls nearly straight down. The spout's length does most of the work, so medieval gargoyles were long. Builders also used many of them: Viollet-le-Duc wrote that splitting the roof's water into many falls left each one a thin thread that could not harm the walls. The first gargoyles appeared around 1220 at Laon; Paris used them everywhere by about 1240. In 1724 London's Building Act required downpipes on new houses.

La Gargouille

Rouen, in Normandy, tells where the first gargoyle came from. Step through the story.

Romanus was bishop of Rouen in the 630s; the dragon first appears in writing in 1394. From about the 12th century until 1790, the chapter of Rouen freed one condemned prisoner every Ascension Day, the privilège de saint Romain, in memory of the man who went with him.

A thousand dragons

The three great halls of the Forbidden City stand on a three-tier terrace of white marble, 8.13 metres high. Under the balustrades sit dragon heads with a hole through each mouth: 1,142 of them, by the Palace Museum's count. In a heavy storm all of them spout at once.

Rain on the terraces: the dragons are spitting.

People in Beijing call it 千龙吐水, a thousand dragons spitting water. Each head is a chi, a hornless dragon, one of the dragon's nine sons, said to hold great quantities of water in its belly. The terrace slopes outward a little so the rain finds the mouths. Across town, the Temple of Heaven's three tiers carry 99 spouts each: clouds on the lowest, phoenixes in the middle, dragons on top.

The face that ate itself

A Hindu story, as Heinrich Zimmer retold it: the demon Rahu came to make a demand of Shiva. Shiva opened his third eye and a lion-headed creature sprang out, starving. Rahu begged for mercy and was spared, which left the creature nothing to eat, so Shiva told it to eat itself. It ate from the tail up until only the face was left. Shiva named it Kirtimukha, face of glory, and set it over the doors of temples.

The same face travelled east with Hindu and Buddhist builders. On Khmer lintels it is Kala, all-devouring time: a great upper jaw, no lower jaw, garlands and vines pouring from its mouth. In Thai it is หน้ากาล, na kan, the one who swallows everything, even itself. On Java the Kala head glares over temple doors with bulging eyes and curved fangs.

Water from a stone

In the Kathmandu Valley, carved stone spouts called hiti (in Newar) or dhunge dhara (in Nepali, stone faucet) sit at the bottom of stepped pits, fed from underground. Patan's Manga Hiti, built in 570, still runs; its water serves the Krishna Mandir every day. Press and hold to fill a pot.

Press and hold the picture.

Most spouts are a makara: a crocodile's snout, an elephant's trunk, a boar's tusks and ears, a peacock's tail, its body forming the open channel. In a 2019 survey, 224 of the valley's spouts on record still gave water. A European gargoyle throws water away from a building; a hiti brings it to people.

Carve one

Every gargoyle was a block of stone first. Pick one and chip it out. Sound on for the chisel.

At Gloucester Cathedral the master mason first models the figure full size in clay, and the masons copy it into the stone with a pointing machine, then by eye. A trough is cut along the back so the water leaves by the mouth. Each of Paisley Abbey's twelve new gargoyles took about four weeks; one grotesque at York Minster took fourteen.

Find the astronaut

In 1992 the Puerta de Ramos of Salamanca's New Cathedral was restored for the next year's Las Edades del Hombre exhibition, and the stonemason Miguel Romero carved a few things of his own time among the old ones: an astronaut, and nearby a smiling dragon with a three-scoop ice cream. Find both.

Stories

APK · CC BY 4.0Washington, 1984–1986

Darth Vader on the cathedral

Washington National Cathedral asked children, through National Geographic World, to design a grotesque; about 1,400 entered from 16 countries. Christopher Rader, 13, drew Darth Vader and was one of three runners-up. Jay Hall Carpenter modelled it in clay and Patrick J. Plunkett carved it high on the northwest tower, where it takes binoculars to find.

Washington, 1986

An umbrella

The grand prize went to Alison Garner, 12, for a toothy man holding an umbrella. She had read that rainwater drains out of the mouths of gargoyles, so she gave her grotesque an umbrella for protection.

Colin · CC BY-SA 3.0Paisley, 1990s

The alien of Paisley Abbey

David Lindsay carved twelve new gargoyles for the abbey's roof, about four weeks each, every one with a channel for water and drawn from 20th-century culture. One looks like the creature from Alien. A photo of it went round the world in 2013.

Gordon Hatton · CC BY-SA 2.0Lincoln, 1250–1280

The Lincoln Imp

A small grotesque sits high in the Angel Choir of Lincoln Cathedral. Its legend, put into verse in 1897: the devil and the wind came to the minster; the devil went in to chat with the Dean, and the wind has been waiting outside ever since.

Fernando Losada Rodríguez · CC BY-SA 4.0Paris, 1726

Notre-Dame loses its gargoyles

Archbishop de Noailles had the roof altered and lead gutters fitted, and most of the medieval gargoyles came down. Those there today are mostly from the 19th-century restoration.

Paris, 1848–1856

The chimeras arrive

Viollet-le-Duc designed the chimeras of the upper gallery himself, and a team of sculptors, Victor Pyanet chief among them, carved them. They are Gothic Revival, not medieval.

Jason Eppink · CC BY 2.0New York, December 1929

On the scaffold

Margaret Bourke-White photographed the Chrysler Building from its scaffolding and was taken with the steel eagle heads. Its ornaments are Nirosta, an 18-8 stainless steel, the first use of that steel in an American building.

Sasha India · CC BY 2.0Bangkok, 1896

A spout from Borobudur

King Chulalongkorn of Siam took eight cartloads of sculpture from Borobudur, with the colonial government's consent: two lions, kala heads, a guardian and one waterspout among them. Some are on show in Bangkok.

Brücke-Osteuropa · CC0Nagoya, 1937

Missing scales

A survey on 6 January 1937 found 58 of the 110 gold scales gone from the male shachihoko. The originals burned in the air raid of 14 May 1945; the pair now on the keep went up when it was rebuilt in 1959. The male is 2.62 m tall and carries 44.69 kg of gold.

Gloucester, 2020

A cheese roller

Gloucester Cathedral's six new working gargoyles show a rugby player, a jockey, a suffragette, a miner, a sheep shearer and a cheese roller.

London, 1240

Downpipes, early

Lead downpipes were already in use on the Tower of London in 1240, to keep spray off its whitewashed walls.

Its names

Most languages name it for the water. English and French name it for the throat.

  • gargouille French from garg-, the sound of gurgling, and goule, a beast's mouth; written gargoule in 1294
  • babewyn Middle English from Italian babbuino, baboon: once used for gargoyles and chimeras alike
  • gárgola Spanish from the French; Spanish garganta, throat, shares the root
  • doccione Italian a protruding gutter
  • Wasserspeier German water-spitter
  • waterspuwer Dutch water-spitter
  • grottesca Italian of the cave: painting found in the buried rooms of Nero's Golden House
  • 螭首 Chinese chīshǒu, hornless-dragon head
  • 鸱吻 Chinese chīwěn, the ridge beast
  • 鬼瓦 Japanese onigawara, ogre tile
  • 鯱 Japanese shachi, the tiger-carp
  • ढुङ्गे धारा Nepali dhunge dhara, stone faucet
  • प्रणाल Sanskrit praṇāla, a temple's water outlet; the makara-praṇāla is India's gargoyle
  • कीर्तिमुख Sanskrit kīrtimukha, face of glory
  • ปนาลี Thai panali, the Thai word for a gargoyle, from praṇāla
  • ตัวสำรอก Thai tua samrok, the one that spits out: a name for the makara
  • หน้ากาล Thai na kan, the face of Kala
